Jovitah Namutebi

PhD Student in Economics and Public Policy
Tufts University

I am a development economist. My research studies how liquidity constraints shape the opportunities open to poor households: not only whether people can afford to migrate or invest, but what kind of opportunity they get when they do. I work on women's labor migration from Uganda to the Gulf, and on how families share resources, including how households invest in children they foster.

My advisors are Margaret McMillan and Douglas Gollin. Before Tufts, I completed an MSc in Mathematics, Economics and Statistics at the African School of Economics and a BA in Development Economics at Makerere University.

Research

Working Papers

Internal Migration and Informal Insurance within Family Networks

Abstract

Migrants in sub-Saharan Africa remain embedded in extended family networks that serve as informal insurance where formal financial markets are limited. Using the Kagera Health and Development Survey, a panel that tracks individuals in Tanzania across nearly two decades of internal migration, I study risk sharing within extended families that span several households and locations. I reject full insurance: households' own shocks reduce their consumption growth. But shocks to other members of the network also affect a household's consumption, consistent with redistribution across households. I also ask whether migrants absorb shocks hitting their relatives, and whether this burden differs between women and men.

Research in Progress

The Price of Opportunity: Financial Constraints and Job Allocation in International Migration

Abstract

Most research on liquidity constraints and migration asks whether poor households can afford to migrate at all. I study what happens after they do. Ugandan women recruited as domestic workers for Saudi Arabia can either pay their recruitment costs upfront or have the agency finance them and repay from their wages. I ask whether the way a woman finances her migration shapes the job and salary she ends up with. The project combines placement records, a phone survey of returned migrants, an experiment with recruitment officers, and a model of how agencies allocate jobs, with implications for recruitment-fee regulation and the ILO Employer Pays Principle.

The Price of Kinship: Free Primary Schooling and Investment in Fostered Children in Uganda

Abstract

About one in five Ugandan children lives away from their parents with relatives. Before 1997, fostered children were less likely to attend school than the host family's own children in the same household. I use Uganda's introduction of Universal Primary Education in 1997, and census data from before and after, to ask whether this gap reflects the price of schooling or households' preferences for their own children.

Who Insures a Shocked Spouse? Individual versus Household-Based Risk Sharing in Extended Families

Abstract

Using the Indonesia Family Life Survey (1993–2014), I test whether a health shock to one spouse draws support from that spouse's own relatives rather than from the couple's pooled networks, comparing the two families within the same couple.
